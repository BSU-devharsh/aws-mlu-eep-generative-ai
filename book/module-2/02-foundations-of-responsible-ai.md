# Chapter 2: Foundations of Responsible AI

This chapter defines responsible AI and shows how organizations practice it. It names the dimensions of responsible AI, follows responsibility across the lifecycle, walks through a risk assessment, introduces the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and compares two articles of the EU AI Act.

## Why it matters

Evaluation (Chapter 1) tells you how a model behaves. Responsible AI tells you how
it *should* behave, and how to build an organization that delivers that. This
chapter defines responsible AI, introduces its dimensions, shows that
responsibility spans the whole design-build-operate lifecycle, and gives a
concrete method for assessing the risk of an AI application.

## What is responsible AI?

Responsible AI has a working definition that the rest of the module relies on.

```{admonition} Definition
:class: tip
**Responsible AI** is the practice of designing, developing, and using AI
technology with the goal of maximizing benefits and minimizing risks and
unintended harms.
```

In practice, responsible AI is defined through a core set of **dimensions** that
an organization assesses and updates over time as the technology evolves. Three
points are easy to miss:

- Responsible AI is an **organizational structure, a set of principles, and a
  practice**, not a subdomain of AI you can delegate to one team.
- The dimensions **depend on the organization** and its responsible-AI maturity.
- **New dimensions can emerge** as new scientific evidence appears.

Why so much focus on it? Because building and maintaining customer trust is a top
priority, and generative AI introduces new failure modes: hallucinations and
inaccuracies, instructions that leak private information, biased or hateful text,
and unlicensed or unlawful content. A regulatory landscape is actively developing
to understand and mitigate these risks.

## The responsible AI dimensions

AWS frames responsible AI around eight dimensions. Chapter 3 explores each in
depth; here is the map:

```{list-table}
:header-rows: 1
:widths: 26 74

* - Dimension
  - In one line
* - **Controllability**
  - Mechanisms to monitor and steer AI system behavior.
* - **Privacy & Security**
  - Appropriately obtaining, using, and protecting data and models.
* - **Safety**
  - Preventing harmful system output and misuse.
* - **Fairness**
  - Considering impacts on different groups of stakeholders.
* - **Veracity & Robustness**
  - Achieving correct outputs, even with unexpected or adversarial inputs.
* - **Explainability**
  - Understanding and evaluating outputs generated by an AI system.
* - **Transparency**
  - Enabling stakeholders to make informed choices about engaging with the system.
* - **Governance**
  - Incorporating best practices across the AI supply chain, providers and
    deployers alike.
```

## Responsibility spans the whole lifecycle

No matter which part of the lifecycle you work on, **design**, **build**, or
**operate**, you should always consider responsible AI.

**Design.** Discuss the use case with diverse stakeholders, evaluate whether AI
actually adds value, and conduct a thorough risk assessment of the proposed use
case.

**Build.** Verify training data is safe, relevant, and representative; consider
legal requirements (licensing, privacy, consent); use metrics with confidence
intervals to evaluate outcomes; and apply safeguards, value alignment, and (where
appropriate) model disgorgement to mitigate risk.

**Operate.** Give end users a way to inquire about outputs for high-risk use cases
and be transparent about limitations; check for **model drift** as the world
changes; and ensure the model is used as intended (a model trained on US data
should be used for the US context).

## Assessing the risk of an AI application

Risk assessment is a structured, three-step process:

1. **Define** the use case and the relevant stakeholders.
2. **Identify** harmful events and evaluate both **inherent** and **residual**
   risk.
3. **Summarize** risk levels across all dimensions and conclude findings.

### Quantifying risk: likelihood and severity

Following the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, risk is quantified along two
axes:

- **Likelihood**: how probable an event is.
- **Severity**: the magnitude of its consequences.

Each is scored on a scale. Likelihood runs from *Highly unlikely* (less than once
per decade) through *Possible* to *Frequent* (more than 100 times a year).
Severity, for a given dimension, runs from *Very low* to *Extreme*. For the
veracity dimension, for instance, *Very low* severity is negligible
hallucination, while *Extreme* is persuasive, dangerous output causing
irreversible real-world harm.

Combining the two in a matrix yields an overall rating:

```{list-table} Likelihood x Severity (illustrative)
:header-rows: 1
:widths: 20 16 16 16 16 16

* - Likelihood \\ Severity
  - Very low
  - Low
  - Moderate
  - Major
  - Extreme
* - **Frequent**
  - Low
  - Medium
  - High
  - Critical
  - Critical
* - **Possible**
  - Very Low
  - Low
  - Medium
  - High
  - Critical
* - **Highly unlikely**
  - Very Low
  - Very Low
  - Very Low
  - Low
  - High
```

```{admonition} Worked example: a medical triage assistant
:class: note
For a symptom-checking assistant, a veracity failure (a confident but wrong
suggestion) is *Possible* in likelihood and *Major-to-Extreme* in severity,
landing it at **High** or **Critical** risk. That rating tells you to add human
oversight, strong disclaimers, and tight guardrails before launch, the techniques
of Chapter 4.
```

## The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0

The likelihood-and-severity approach above comes from the **NIST AI Risk
Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)**, a voluntary framework published by the U.S.
National Institute of Standards and Technology in January 2023 to help
organizations manage the risks of AI systems. It is worth knowing in its own right
because it has become a common reference point for responsible-AI governance.

### Characteristics of trustworthy AI

The framework defines AI risk as a function of the **likelihood** of an event and
the **magnitude (severity) of its impact**, and it organizes "trustworthiness"
into seven characteristics. They map closely onto this module's dimensions:

```{list-table}
:header-rows: 1
:widths: 42 58

* - NIST trustworthiness characteristic
  - Related dimension in this book
* - Valid and reliable
  - Veracity and robustness ({doc}`03-dimensions-of-responsible-ai`)
* - Safe
  - Safety
* - Secure and resilient
  - Privacy and security; robustness
* - Accountable and transparent
  - Transparency; governance
* - Explainable and interpretable
  - Explainability
* - Privacy-enhanced
  - Privacy and security
* - Fair, with harmful bias managed
  - Fairness
```

### The four core functions

The AI RMF organizes practice into four functions, which align with the
design-build-operate lifecycle above:

```{list-table}
:header-rows: 1

* - Function
  - What it covers
* - **Govern**
  - A culture of risk management: policies, accountability, roles, and oversight
    that cut across the other three functions.
* - **Map**
  - Establish context and identify risks: the use case, stakeholders, and where
    harms could arise (the "define and identify" steps above).
* - **Measure**
  - Analyze, assess, and track risks using quantitative and qualitative methods
    (the evaluation of {doc}`01-evaluating-llms` and the likelihood x severity
    rating).
* - **Manage**
  - Prioritize and act on risks: allocate resources, apply mitigations
    (guardrails, oversight), and monitor over time.
```

## The EU AI Act: Article 50 versus Article 15

Two articles of the EU AI Act {cite}`eu2024aiact` touch almost every generative AI application, and they are easy to confuse. Article 50 is about transparency: telling people that they are dealing with AI and marking what AI generates. Article 15 is about engineering quality: how accurate, robust, and secure a high-risk system must be. Article 50 applies to many systems at any risk level, while Article 15 applies only to high-risk systems but reaches into how they are built and tested. The figure below sets the two side by side.

```{image} ../_static/figures/bk_art50_vs_art15.*

The Article 15 lane shows a high-risk system whose provider declares accuracy levels and metrics, adds a fail-safe backup, shields the model from data poisoning and adversarial inputs, and passes a conformity assessment before the system goes on the market.
:width: 95%
:align: center
:class: anim-fig
```

### Article 50: tell people and mark the output

Article 50 has applied since August 2, 2026, and like the rest of the Act it covers providers outside the EU whose systems' output is used in the EU {cite}`ec2026art50`. It sets four duties, two for providers and two for deployers.

1. **Interaction.** A provider must design a system that talks with people, such as a chatbot or an agent, so that people are told they are dealing with AI, unless that is obvious. A company that builds an assistant on Amazon Bedrock and offers it under its own name is the provider of that assistant.
2. **Marking.** A provider of a system that generates synthetic audio, images, video, or text must mark the output in a machine-readable format so that it can be detected as AI-generated. The marking must be effective, interoperable, robust, and reliable as far as technically feasible.
3. **Emotion recognition and biometric categorization.** A deployer of such a system must inform the people exposed to it.
4. **Deepfakes and public-interest text.** A deployer that publishes a deepfake must disclose that it is artificial. A deployer that publishes AI-generated text to inform the public on matters of public interest must disclose that too, unless a person reviewed the text and holds editorial responsibility for it.

The disclosure must be clear and must come at the latest at the first interaction or exposure. Fines for breaking these duties reach 15 million euros or 3 percent of worldwide annual turnover. The 2026 Digital Omnibus gave providers of generative systems already on the market before August 2, 2026 until December 2, 2026 to meet the marking duty; the other three duties had no delay {cite}`eu2026omnibus`. The Commission's Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, published on June 10, 2026 and found adequate in July, expects at least two layers of machine-readable marking, such as signed metadata and a watermark. Its non-binding guidelines of July 20, 2026 explain what falls in and out of scope {cite}`ec2026transparencycode,ec2026art50guidelines`.

Marking is partly a model feature and partly your job. Some models in Amazon Bedrock add a watermark to their output: the Bedrock user guide lists the Amazon Nova Sonic speech models, which add an inaudible watermark to all the speech they generate, and several Anthropic Claude models, which watermark text {cite}`aws2026watermarking`. Not every model adds one, and editing, paraphrasing, translating, compressing, or cropping can weaken a watermark, so check what your pipeline keeps. The Content Credentials check in {doc}`04-improving-security-and-safety` shows how easily ordinary image code drops signed metadata.

### Article 15: accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity

Article 15 applies to high-risk systems, such as AI used in hiring, credit scoring, or education, from December 2, 2027 for the uses listed in Annex III and from August 2, 2028 for products covered by Annex I {cite}`ec2026art15`. The provider must design the system to reach an appropriate level of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity and keep that level throughout the lifecycle. Each requirement maps to work in this module.

- **Accuracy.** Declare the accuracy levels and the metrics used to measure them in the instructions for use. The evaluation methods in {doc}`01-evaluating-llms` produce those numbers.
- **Robustness.** Tolerate errors, faults, and inconsistencies, for example with redundancy and fail-safe plans. A system that keeps learning after release must stop its own possibly biased outputs from feeding back into future training.
- **Cybersecurity.** Resist attempts to alter the system's use, outputs, or performance. The Act names data poisoning, model poisoning through pre-trained components, adversarial examples, confidentiality attacks, and model flaws. The jailbreak defenses, guardrails, and adversarial machine learning material in {doc}`04-improving-security-and-safety` apply here.

Article 15 binds the provider, which shows compliance in a conformity assessment before the system goes on the market. Other duties that often appear beside it come from neighboring articles: risk management (Article 9), logging (Article 12), human oversight (Article 14), and the quality management system (Article 17); a deployer's duties are in Article 26. For a product with digital elements, meeting the essential requirements of the Cyber Resilience Act counts toward the cybersecurity part of Article 15, but not toward accuracy or robustness {cite}`eu2024cra`.

```{list-table}
:header-rows: 1
:widths: 20 40 40

* - Aspect
  - Article 50
  - Article 15
* - What it governs
  - Telling people about AI and marking AI output
  - How well a high-risk system works and resists attack
* - Which systems
  - Interactive, generative, emotion recognition, and biometric categorization systems, and deepfakes, at any risk level
  - High-risk systems only
* - Who must act
  - Providers for 50(1) and 50(2); deployers for 50(3) and 50(4)
  - The provider, before the system is placed on the market
* - Applies from
  - August 2, 2026; December 2, 2026 for marking by generative systems already on the market
  - December 2, 2027 (Annex III); August 2, 2028 (Annex I)
```

Both articles can apply to one application. A hiring assistant built on Bedrock is high-risk under Annex III, so Article 15 governs its accuracy and security, and its chat interface must still tell applicants that they are talking to AI under Article 50(1). A marketing image generator is not high-risk, so it has no Article 15 duties, but its provider must mark its images under Article 50(2). The companion textbook covers both articles in more depth ([Article 50 and Article 15](https://book.com.puter.tips/chapters/17_emerging/chapter17.html#article-50-and-article-15-transparency-versus-accuracy-robustness-and-cybersecurity)).

## Social scoring: the Article 5 line for Bedrock apps

Article 50 and Article 15 regulate systems that may be built. Article 5 lists practices that may not be built at all, and social scoring is the one a generative AI team is most likely to drift into by accident. A foundation model can read support chats, reviews, or social media posts and return a rating, so a score built from data that was collected for a different purpose takes only a prompt. This section explains where the EU AI Act draws the line and how to stay on the right side of it. The figure below walks through the test.

```{image} ../_static/figures/bk_social_scoring.*
:alt: A decision flow for Article 5(1)(c) of the EU AI Act. Step 1, the system scores people over time from social behavior or personal or personality traits. Step 2, the score leads to worse treatment. Step 3, either test is enough, an unrelated context where data from one setting is used against the person in another, or treatment out of proportion to the behavior or its gravity. Step 4, if so, the practice has been prohibited since February 2, 2025 for public and private actors. Step 5, if neither test is met, the score is not banned but still regulated, since credit scoring is high-risk under Annex III with duties from December 2, 2027 and GDPR Article 22 limits decisions based solely on an automated score. Step 6 compares a banned score, the tone of a customer's support chats used to deny them a loan, with a regulated one, a loan assistant that scores creditworthiness from financial data.
:width: 95%
:align: center
:class: anim-fig
```

### The test in Article 5(1)(c\)

Article 5(1)(c\) bans placing on the market, putting into service, or using an AI system that evaluates or classifies people over a certain period, based on their social behavior or on known, inferred, or predicted personal or personality characteristics, when the resulting score leads to detrimental or unfavorable treatment in either of two ways {cite}`eu2024aiact`.

1. **Unrelated context.** The treatment happens in a social context unrelated to the one where the data was generated or collected.
2. **Out of proportion.** The treatment is unjustified or disproportionate to the behavior or its gravity.

One of the two is enough. The ban has applied since February 2, 2025, and breaking it can cost up to 35 million euros or 7 percent of worldwide annual turnover, the Act's highest tier of fines. The Commission's guidelines on prohibited practices, published on February 4, 2025, confirm that the ban applies whether a public body or a private company provides or uses the system {cite}`ec2025prohibitedguidelines,fpf2026socialscoring`.

Many summaries still say the ban covers only public authorities. That was the Commission's 2021 proposal, which banned social scoring by or on behalf of public authorities that judged people's trustworthiness {cite}`ec2021aiactproposal`. The Council extended the ban to private actors in its compromise text of November 2021, and the enacted Act keeps that reach: its Recital 31 warns that social scoring by public or private actors may lead to discriminatory outcomes and the exclusion of certain groups.

### What stays legal, and what still applies

Scoring itself is not banned. A lender's credit score built from relevant financial data falls outside Article 5(1)(c\) as long as the treatment it leads to is justified and proportionate {cite}`fpf2026socialscoring`. Such a system is high-risk instead: AI that evaluates creditworthiness or sets a credit score is listed in Annex III, so Article 15 and the other high-risk duties apply to it from December 2, 2027 {cite}`eu2026omnibus`. Data protection law applies on top. In SCHUFA, the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) held in 2023 that a credit agency's automated score counts as a decision under Article 22 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) when the lender draws strongly on it to grant or refuse credit {cite}`cjeu2023schufa`. In Dun & Bradstreet Austria in 2025, the same court held that a person refused a contract on the strength of an automated credit assessment may require an explanation of the procedure and principles actually applied, under GDPR Article 15(1)(h); handing over the algorithm alone is not enough {cite}`cjeu2025dnb`.

The difference between the two cases in the figure is the source and use of the data. A loan assistant that rates creditworthiness from income, debts, and repayment history is high-risk and must meet those duties. The same assistant, asked to downgrade applicants whose support chats sound rude, uses data from one context to treat people worse in another, which is exactly what Article 5(1)(c\) prohibits.

### Designing a Bedrock application that scores people

When a Bedrock application rates, ranks, or classifies people, a few design rules keep it reviewable.

1. **Inventory the scores.** List every model output that rates or classifies a person or a group, including risk flags, tiers, and segments, not only numeric scores.
2. **Limit inputs by purpose.** Pass the model only the fields that belong to the decision, and record where each one was collected. Retrieval that pulls a customer's whole history into the prompt can bring in data from unrelated contexts without anyone deciding to.
3. **Match the consequence to the behavior.** Write down what each score triggers and check that the response fits the behavior it measures.
4. **Watch for proxies.** Postcodes, names, language, and nationality can stand in for protected characteristics. The Dutch childcare benefits scandal, in which the tax authority used dual nationality as a fraud risk indicator, ended in a 2.75 million euro fine in December 2021 {cite}`iapp2021dutchdpa`.
5. **Keep it explainable and contestable.** Log the inputs and the prompt version behind each score so that you can explain the procedure and principles applied, and put a person with real authority in the loop where GDPR Article 22 requires it.

The companion textbook traces the Chinese Social Credit System, the Dutch SyRI ruling, and the case law in more depth ([Social Scoring: Where the EU AI Act Draws the Line](https://book.com.puter.tips/chapters/17_emerging/chapter17.html#social-scoring-where-the-eu-ai-act-draws-the-line)).

#### Check your understanding: social scoring

Try this question before you open the answer. A rental platform in the EU uses an AI model to score applicants from their restaurant reviews and social media activity, and rejects those with low scores. Under the EU AI Act, how is this best classified?

- **A.** Allowed, because the ban on social scoring covers only public authorities
- **B.** Prohibited social scoring, because data from unrelated social contexts leads to detrimental treatment
- **C.** High-risk credit scoring that needs only a conformity assessment
- **D.** Outside the Act, because the platform is a private company

```{admonition} Answer
:class: dropdown
**B.** Restaurant reviews and social media posts were created in contexts that have nothing to do with renting a home, and a rejection is detrimental treatment, so the unrelated-context test in Article 5(1)(c\) is met. A is the 2021 draft, not the enacted Act, which covers public and private actors alike, so D is wrong for the same reason. C does not fit because this is not a creditworthiness assessment built from financial data, and no conformity assessment can make a prohibited practice lawful.
```

## In the news

Responsible AI has moved from voluntary principle to emerging regulation, with
frameworks such as the EU AI Act and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework shaping
how organizations classify and govern AI by risk level. The dimensions in this
chapter map closely onto these regimes, which is why treating responsible AI as a
governance practice, rather than a feature, increasingly aligns with legal
obligation, not just good intentions.

The companion textbook covers the legal side in more depth: the EU AI Act's risk tiers and its duties for providers and deployers, the 2026 Digital Omnibus that moved the high-risk deadlines to December 2027 and August 2028, ISO/IEC 42001, and NIST's newer profiles ([Governing AI: Laws, Standards, and Roles](https://book.com.puter.tips/chapters/17_emerging/chapter17.html#governing-ai-laws-standards-and-roles)).

## Key takeaways

The points below summarize the foundations that the next two chapters build on.

- **Responsible AI** maximizes benefit and minimizes harm, defined through
  evolving **dimensions** and practiced organization-wide.
- It spans the **design-build-operate** lifecycle; everyone is responsible.
- **Risk assessment** is define -> identify and evaluate -> summarize, with risk
  quantified as **likelihood x severity** per the NIST framework.
- **Governance** ties the practice together across teams.
- In the EU AI Act, **Article 50** requires disclosure and machine-readable marking of AI
  output at any risk level, while **Article 15** requires accuracy, robustness, and
  cybersecurity from high-risk systems.
- **Social scoring** is banned under Article 5(1)(c\) for public and private actors
  when a score leads to worse treatment in an unrelated context or out of proportion
  to the behavior; credit scoring from relevant financial data is high-risk instead.
